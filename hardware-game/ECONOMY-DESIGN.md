# Economy Design Research: Competitive Hardware-Tycoon Rounds Game

Six areas, each with options → tradeoffs → recommendation.

## 1. Market / pricing design — how to price N commodities so no meta build dominates

The core insight from all four prior-art games: **you don't balance the builds, you let prices balance them.** If hashes/tokens/frames are priced by inverse supply, the "best" build is by definition the one everyone else *isn't* running. Meta-chasing becomes self-defeating. The job is picking the price function, not hand-tuning GPU stats.

**Option A — Victoria 3-style order-book ratio pricing (recommended).**
`price = basePrice × (1 + k × (demand − supply)/max(demand, supply))`, clamped (Vic3 clamps at ±75% of base so nothing goes free or infinite). Demand comes from NPC "buyers" (the in-game economy: an AI colony/company that consumes spreadsheets, tokens, frames at a drifting base rate — exactly OTC's colony, which consumes resources and *builds toward whatever is cheap*, auto-stabilizing the market). Supply = total player production this tick. Deterministic, one formula per commodity, trivially computable in a contract or a cron tick, no order matching needed. Vic3's one wart to avoid: it lets bought ≠ sold and creates/destroys money from the gap — in a zero-sum competition, make the NPC buyer the money faucet and let it absorb the imbalance explicitly. Sources: [Vic3 Market wiki](https://vic3.paradoxwikis.com/Market), [Paradox deep-dive on Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/deep-dive-modeling-the-global-economy-in-victoria-3).

**Option B — OTC-style transaction-driven random-walk market.** In Offworld Trading Company, *production doesn't move price — only trades do*: roughly every 10 units sold drops price ~$1, with elasticity scaling by price level (selling into a $200 market moves it 2–3× more than a $30 market), plus a base demand drift upward over time (`0.001 × players × (seconds+100)`) and a growing NPC colony as demand backstop. This is the best-feeling real-time version: you can *hold inventory* and time your dumps, sell rivals' markets down, corner and squeeze. It's the "study closely" game for a reason — the entire game is the market screen. But it needs continuous ticks and inventory-timing UI; more sim complexity. Sources: [OTC Market wiki](https://offworldtradingcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Market), [Steam guide: OTC strategic concepts](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=1146344676), [forum: the economic model](https://forums.offworldgame.com/476730/the-economic-model).

**Option C — EVE-style pure player order book.** Players post bids/asks on commodities; no NPC pricing at all. Deepest possible market game, but it needs *large* player counts for liquidity, is manipulable at small N (two colluders can wash-trade), and requires sink/faucet discipline (EVE runs monthly economic reports and historically employed a full-time economist to keep ISK sinks ≈ faucets). Wrong for a round-based game with 10–500 players. Sources: [EVE economy analysis](https://050nor.substack.com/p/eve-online-economic-system-design-player-motication-emotion), [ISK sinks/faucets](https://fastercapital.com/content/ISK-Sink-or-ISK-Faucet--The-Economic-Balance-in-EVE-Online.html).

**Recommendation:** Option A for pricing, with two OTC steals grafted on: (1) sales move the price, not production — so players who can warehouse output and time sales have a skill lever; (2) an NPC demand curve that *grows over the round* and shifts toward cheap commodities, so a crashed market self-heals and late-round demand is bigger than early-round (comeback fuel). Add M.U.L.E.'s store as a floor: an NPC that always buys at a low base price, so no commodity is ever worth literally zero ([M.U.L.E. auction mechanics](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/M.U.L.E./Phase_III:_The_auction)).

**Tuning rule of thumb:** set price-decay elasticity so that the Nth player entering a commodity earns roughly what the 1st player in the (N/k)th-most-popular commodity earns. If 60% of players mine hashes, hash income per rig should fall ~40–50% below a mono-price baseline. Publish the formula — the metagame of reading the market IS the game.

## 2. Round structure

**Length.** Dark Forest settled on **4–10 day rounds** (9 days typical; Round 4 was a 4-day "lightning round") after iterating across v0.5/v0.6 — long enough for strategy arcs, short enough that a blown start doesn't mean weeks of zombie play ([v0.6 round wrapups](https://blog.zkga.me/v6-r3-wrapup)). Options:
- **Daily (24h):** high pace, low depth; market never gets interesting because nobody has time to react to price shifts. Good for a "blitz" side mode only.
- **Weekly (recommended):** matches Dark Forest's proven cadence, fits a "check in twice a day, adjust build, time your sells" play pattern, and weekly seasons map naturally to onchain prize epochs and a live-crawled catalog refresh (real prices change week to week — each round's catalog snapshot is fresh content for free).
- **Monthly seasons of weekly rounds:** season-long leaderboard across 4 weekly rounds gives persistence without carryover-wealth snowballing (each round resets budget — the M.U.L.E. lesson below).

**Buying model — the biggest single design decision:**
- **Open market (everyone can buy the same 5090):** simple, fair, fully parallel, works onchain trivially. But it collapses into a solved build-order the moment someone publishes the spreadsheet — the only counter-pressure is output-price decay, which pushes toward mixed builds but doesn't create *drafting* tension.
- **Limited supply + escalating price (recommended): the Power Grid resource market.** In Power Grid, resources sit on a priced track — cheap slots sell first, so the more of a resource the table buys, the more the next buyer pays, and buy order is rank-based (leader buys *last*, at the worst prices — a built-in rubber band). Translate directly: the round's catalog has **K units of each SKU on a price ladder above real street price**; each purchase climbs the ladder. Everyone *can* get a 5090, but the 8th buyer pays 1.6×, which makes the crawled real price a *starting* price, not the price, and makes "what is everyone else buying?" the central read. Sources: [Games Precipice Power Grid analysis](https://www.gamesprecipice.com/powergrid/), [Power Grid rules](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/power-grid/game-rules.php).
- **Snake draft / auction phases:** M.U.L.E. and Power Grid both auction scarce assets, and both learned the same lesson — M.U.L.E. originally auctioned *all* land and had to stop because the leader bought everything and the game was decided by midgame; the fix was granting each player a free plot per turn and auctioning only the surplus ([Digital Antiquarian on Dan Bunten](https://www.filfre.net/2013/02/dan-bunten-and-m-u-l-e/)). A synchronous draft also forces everyone online at once — poison for a web/onchain game. Use auctions only for a handful of *chase* items per round (one Blackwell B200, one exotic), not the base catalog.

**Recommendation:** weekly rounds, same fixed fake budget every round (no carryover — fresh-start-per-round is what keeps season 3 joinable), open catalog with Power Grid price ladders on constrained SKUs, plus 2–3 auctioned unicorn parts. Consider Power Grid's other famous mechanic for the payout table: **diminishing returns on scale** — powering city #20 pays $2 more than #19 vs ~$15 for the first — as a template for facility-level income concavity (see §4).

## 3. Anti-degenerate-meta mechanics

- **Dynamic prices are the primary defense** (§1). Everything else is a secondary constraint that makes "just buy 8 of the best GPU" physically illegal, not just unprofitable.
- **Watts as the second budget (recommended, and it's free content):** every real part has a real TDP. Give each player a power feed (say 1500W base, upgradable transformers at escalating cost) and charge fake electricity per tick per watt. This single constraint recreates real perf-per-watt tradeoffs — a 5090 is not strictly better than two 4070s once watts are scarce — and it's *thematically true*, which is the whole charm of the real-catalog concept. It's literally Power Grid's theme worn honestly. Electricity cost can itself be a dynamic market (total fleet draw raises the tariff) → a soft global cap on production inflation, i.e., the money sink (EVE's lesson: without aggressive sinks, round-end scores just inflate; best sinks are ones players opt into for value — power, cooling, repairs qualify).
- **Rock-paper-scissors between outputs:** don't hand-author it — derive it from real benchmark axes. Hashes want raw GPU compute, tokens want VRAM bandwidth/capacity, spreadsheets want single-core CPU, video frames want GPU+RAM balance. Real silicon already has RPS baked in (an EPYC is a spreadsheet monster and a terrible miner). Then commodity price decay makes the *population's* build distribution the RPS opponent. Optional spice: weekly demand events ("the colony renders a film this round — frame demand ×2") = OTC's random events / M.U.L.E.'s production events, which exist to shake locals out of equilibrium.
- **Depreciation / failure:** real hardware angle again — parts lose resale value per tick (depreciation, forces reinvestment decisions) and have a small failure hazard scaled by load (run at 100% duty = more risk; underclocking as a real strategy). Keep failure *deterministic-ish or insurable* if onchain (pure RNG losses feel terrible and are oracle-hard; prefer "wear meter fills by usage, part dies at 100, maintenance spend resets it" — that's a sink, a decision, and no dice). Space/cooling as a third constraint (rack slots; heat = sum of watts, over-cap throttles output) is worth having but keep it coarse — two constraints (money, watts) already produce a rich knapsack; three tightens; four is spreadsheet torture.

## 4. Idle-game math — why linear production + dynamic prices

Kongregate's canonical idle-math series ([The Math of Idle Games, Part I](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-math-of-idle-games-part-i)) defines the solo-idle template: **exponential cost growth vs linear/polynomial production growth**, so each new generator tier matters and the wall keeps receding. That template is built for a *solo* game where the only opponent is the wall. In a competitive round:

- **Exponential production is fatal:** any compounding loop (income → buy more producers → more income) makes the round decided by whoever compounds first; a 5% early edge becomes 2× by day 4, and days 5–7 are dead time. This is exactly M.U.L.E.'s land-auction failure ("outcome a foregone conclusion halfway through") generalized. Competitive-balance analyses agree: linear scaling is the tool "for competitive games... where power ceilings are intentional" ([linear vs exponential in games](https://missionszanx.com/guides/linear-vs-exponential-growth-in-games)).
- **The fix is structural, not tuning:** (1) **fixed budget, no reinvestment of earnings into hardware** — earnings are *score*, hardware comes only from the round budget (plus maybe small scheduled stipends, M.U.L.E.'s free-plot-per-turn move). Production is then linear in time by construction: `score(t) = Σ machines × rate × price(t)`, and all skill lives in the price(t) term — build selection, sell timing, pivots. Or (2) allow reinvestment but make income concave in fleet size (Power Grid's payout table: 20th city earns $2 vs 1st city's $15) *and* let price decay tax popular outputs — softer, still needs careful tuning to kill compounding.
- **Ticks:** flat per-tick output, hourly ticks are plenty for a weekly round (168 ticks — enough resolution for price dynamics, few enough to settle cheaply, see §5). No offline penalty, no clicking multipliers — the "idle" part is genuinely idle; the *active* part is market reads and mid-round pivots (selling a rig at depreciated value to chase a price spike must be possible, or the round has one decision at t=0 and is a spreadsheet contest, not a game).

**Recommendation:** fixed budget + earnings-as-score (structure 1), hourly ticks, linear production, all dynamism in prices. If playtests say "one decision then wait," add the stipend drip (fresh budget at day 2, 4, 6) so there are 3–4 forced re-decisions per round against a moved market.

## 5. Onchain feasibility

Prior art says round-based economy competitions are the *proven* onchain genre: **Dark Forest** (weekly-ish rounds, NFT prize planets, scoring by round-specific objective — [blog.zkga.me](https://blog.zkga.me/)), **0xMonaco** (players deploy strategy *contracts* that race; ELO + leaderboard identity created a reputation game that pulled in elite devs; the meta was live because you could redeploy between races — [Matchbox DAO writeup](https://mirror.xyz/matchboxdao.eth/-xVvvo36y-LHvrW99heb4VJ-xAOWQ-BQAx6MCwHLWnk)), **Primodium / Sky Strife** on MUD (persistent resource loops, burner-wallet UX so it feels like a browser game — [WASD on Primodium](https://wasd.mirror.xyz/uiJorhoqRort9qQHvEp3kSSgtTcbW54LZXnW52mstsE), [Lattice](https://lattice.xyz/about)).

Three architectures:
- **Fully onchain ticks (MUD-style):** every hourly tick a contract updates supply, prices, balances. Honest but pointless here — game state per player is tiny (a parts list + timestamps), and production between actions is a *pure function of time*. You don't need to execute ticks; you need to **compute production lazily**: on any player action (buy/sell/pivot) or at settlement, integrate `rate × price(t)` since their last action. This is the standard staking-rewards accumulator pattern — prices only change at discrete market events, so the integral is a sum over price epochs. Fully onchain, zero cron.
- **Onchain settlement only, offchain sim:** server runs ticks, posts a merkle root of final scores; prizes claimable onchain. Cheapest, but then "onchain" is just a payout rail — you lose the actual win, which is that **0xMonaco-style bot play becomes a feature**: if the market and catalog are contracts, people write trading bots as strategies, and that's the hardcore audience (the agent-esports angle composes directly — AI agents competing in the same market as humans).
- **Recommended hybrid:** market + purchases + lazy-accumulator production **onchain** (an L2 — hourly-granularity epochs on 500 players is nothing); the **catalog oracle offchain** — the live crawl (real prices, benchmark numbers, TDP) is inherently offchain data, posted once per round as a signed catalog root before the round opens. One oracle write per week, disputable because it's public data. Round-open snapshot also kills mid-round oracle-manipulation surface. Determinism note: avoid RNG (failure rolls) or use commit-reveal/wear-meters (§3); everything else in this design is already deterministic. Fully-onchain caveat from the FOCG crowd: *no hidden information onchain* — so don't design around secret builds; embrace public builds (you can see everyone's rigs → the market-read game is richer, like Power Grid's open resource track).

## 6. The "employee" idea — AI workers as units

Tycoon prior art: **Startup Company** employees have hidden salary expectations tied to current speed, and the known-good strategy is hiring low-current/high-max speed workers cheap and leveling them ([Startup Company wiki](https://startupcompany.fandom.com/wiki/Getting_Started)) — i.e., workers are *appreciating assets with a wage drag*, vs hardware's depreciating assets with a power drag. Software Inc adds specialization/compatibility (team chemistry, skill mix). The generalizable frame: **hardware = capex + watts; employees = opex + wages**, and a good tycoon makes you blend both.

The novel angle is strong and should be pushed all the way: **employees are real LLM models from a live-crawled model catalog**, exactly parallel to the parts catalog — real names, real $/Mtok as their in-game wage, real benchmark scores (MMLU/SWE-bench/tok-per-sec) as their production stats. Three implementation tiers:

- **Tier 1 — stats only (recommended for v1):** the model is a unit with crawled cost/quality/speed numbers; it produces "AI work" commodities (code reviewed, tickets answered, documents drafted) whose *quality tier* gates which market it can sell into. This alone delivers the design payoff: a second, opex-shaped constraint axis, perfect thematic symmetry with the hardware crawl, and free content every time a lab ships a model (a new frontier model dropping mid-season IS a patch note). Zero API spend.
- **Tier 2 — self-hosted synergy:** open-weight employees can run *on your own hardware* instead of paying per-token wages — burning your VRAM and watts instead of cash. This is the killer crossover mechanic: it welds the two catalogs together (buy the 5090 to mine, or to host your Llama employee?) and mirrors the real build-vs-buy inference decision practitioners actually face. Pure math, still no real API calls.
- **Tier 3 — real calls:** employees actually execute real LLM calls on real micro-tasks, scored by a grader (the house eval — simple-eval — could grade them). Maximally novel, and adjacent to the agent-esports concept, but it imports real money costs, latency, non-determinism, and grader-gaming; it turns an economy game into an agent benchmark. Keep it as a special "pro league" round type, not the base game.

**Recommendation:** ship Tier 1 in the base game with wages as the round's dominant money sink for AI-work builds, add Tier 2 as the mid-season expansion — it's the mechanic no other tycoon has, because no other tycoon's catalog is real.

## Cross-cutting synthesis (the shape to build)

Weekly rounds, fixed identical budget, no earnings-reinvestment (earnings = score). Real-catalog parts on Power Grid price ladders; watts as second budget with a dynamic tariff; wear-meter depreciation. 4–6 commodities priced by clamped supply/demand ratio with a growing, cheap-seeking NPC buyer (OTC's colony) and an always-bid floor (M.U.L.E.'s store); sales move price, production doesn't — so warehousing/sell-timing is a skill. Hourly epochs, lazy-accumulator production onchain on an L2, catalog posted as a signed weekly snapshot, builds public by design, contracts open so bots are first-class players (0xMonaco's lesson: leaderboard + identity + redeployable strategies = a dev metagame that markets itself). LLM employees as the opex mirror of hardware capex, self-hosting as the weld between them.
