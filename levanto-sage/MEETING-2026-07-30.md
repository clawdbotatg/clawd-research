## Architecture — how Sage actually works (new information)

Marco explained the internals, more than the docs disclose:

- **Base model:** an open-weights **Qwen-family** model, **>300B parameters**.
  They kept only the **first layers** ("cut it") and trained a **classifier
  head** on top of the last retained layer.
- **Confidence is a real classifier output** — the slope of the classifier
  curve — *not* a softmax over the token vocabulary. A yesno decision is a
  genuine 2-class output. This is why the probabilities are calibrated and
  thresholdable, which our benchmarks observed empirically.
- **No decode pass at all.** The custom inference stack (built on **SGLang**)
  does encoding + prefill only. This is *why* it cannot generate text — the
  safety property we documented is structural, not a policy.
- ~1 month of inference optimization, including **three levels of batching**.
  The stack is "very hard to run" — their stated reason (besides secret sauce)
  for never open-sourcing or offering local deployment.
- Runs on a **single NVIDIA B300** (~$53k/GPU). So: no local/laptop story, ever.
- **No reasoning by design** — Qwen family chosen specifically for fast
  non-reasoning answers.
- Marco claims it "performs like GPT-5" on an agent benchmark (name garbled in
  transcript — "Agent Karma"; unverified).
- **Base-model swaps are weeks, not years.** If a new open-weights model drops
  (e.g. Kimi K3), they can re-cut and re-head it. Post-fundraise plan: a
  Kimi-based version (much more expensive to train). Considering GLM as a base
  for coding-specific decisions.
  - *Implication for us:* model bumps will happen. OPEN-QUESTIONS.md item 9
    (re-verify calibration when `meta.model` moves) is not hypothetical.
- Confirmed: no Haiku or other second model anywhere in Sage's own stack —
  it's just the cut Qwen + classifier. (The Haiku confusion on the call was
  about Austin's video-chat filler, not Sage.)

## Use cases discussed (Austin's)

1. **onedollaraudit sanitization gate** (see INJECTION.md). Speed is irrelevant
   here — the win is doing prompt-injection detection cheaper than a frontier
   model. Chris pointed to their published injection-detection benchmark.
2. **Voice-agent latency — the one that landed hardest.** In clawd-video-chat,
   the gap between end-of-utterance and the frontier model speaking is filled
   by a Haiku "filler" pass today (see VIDEO-CHAT.md). Sage could be the
   **~250ms gate** that decides, before any big model wakes up: *is he done
   talking? is this addressed to me? should I fill the silence, and with which
   of ~80 canned utterances?* Extension: **group-call turn-taking** — knowing
   when the agent should and shouldn't speak, which prompting alone hasn't
   solved. Marco: "on paper it's perfect."
3. **Model routing** (already covered by demo_router.py) — Austin framed the
   adoption moment as the *effective → efficient* transition: teams build on a
   frontier model first, then hit cost/latency pain, and that's when a
   cascading-decision layer becomes obviously valuable. Chris independently
   proved the pattern in his own video pipeline: fewer Opus iterative loops cut
   his API costs ~50%. Their dream pitch: "no-brainer API to drop into your
   coding harness, halves your costs."
4. **Good Guy Bad Guy** (offline poisonous-or-not photo app) — blocked twice
   over: **no image support** and no local deployment. Best case is an
   online-uses-Sage / offline-uses-local-LLM hybrid, *if* images ever ship.
5. **Evals as entertainment.** Austin pitched "American Gladiators for models":
   house gladiator models vs newcomer challengers running visual, spectator-
   friendly evals (like animated sorting-algorithm visualizations) — normies
   deciding AI procurement can *see* one model beating another. Marco has an
   unlaunched prototype in exactly this shape: a web3 asset ranker racing GPT
   vs Sage head-to-head on ordering speed. He should launch it.
